A Better Union
Research Project

Making Europe work better.

WMSU Europe is an independent research project examining the practical conditions of European integration: how people find employment, exercise their rights, travel, communicate and build a life across national borders.

Through comparative research into institutions, administrative procedures and everyday experience, the project aims to identify where European cooperation can produce tangible improvements, and which reforms could make them possible.

The project

European integration.
Practical consequences.

A right or an opportunity has practical value when citizens can use it. WMSU examines the relationship between Europe's shared ambitions and the national systems through which they are experienced.

The research starts from concrete situations: a graduate looking for a first job abroad, a worker asked for documents before starting employment, or a passenger organising a journey across several countries. These cases provide a way to examine how rules, institutions and services interact, and where their interaction may create obstacles.

Its scope connects employment and job seeking with rail, language and communications. Across these themes, the question is how national differences affect access to opportunities, and whether those differences reflect necessary safeguards, administrative choices or gaps in coordination.

Starting work across the EU.

Rights on paper and access in practice.

If an EU citizen arrives in another member state and receives a job offer today, can they start work tomorrow?

This study examines the distinction between a formally recognised right and its practical exercise. It asks whether an EU citizen who is entitled to work can actually begin employment, receive pay and complete the necessary formalities without avoidable delay, cost or uncertainty.

Residence registration, proof of address, tax identification and social security are examined as interacting procedures. A document requested by an employer may be a legal requirement, an administrative convenience or a mistaken assumption. A lawful alternative may exist yet remain difficult to find or use. The research must establish which situation applies rather than assume that every obstacle reflects the law.

Formal entitlement

What do the applicable rules permit, and which obligations must be met?

Administrative execution

Can a worker and an informed employer complete the necessary steps?

Practical acceptance

What do employers and their onboarding systems require in an actual hiring process?

The proposed comparison covers all 27 EU member states, tracing the first working day, first payroll and the first three months. Its purpose is to locate the gap between formal protection and effective access, explain how that gap arises, and assess reforms that could reduce it while preserving legitimate safeguards.

Methods

Comparative legal and policy analysis, verification against legislation and official guidance, and process tracing of worker and employer obligations. Planned practical verification includes written authority enquiries and interviews with workers, employers and payroll professionals.

Finding work across borders.

From applications to opportunities.

How can European cooperation make the search for work more accessible, transparent and effective?

This proposed study focuses on recent graduates and other job seekers navigating applications, recruitment platforms and unfamiliar national labour markets. It begins with a concern about the difficulty of securing employment: whether application processes are becoming more burdensome, time to employment is lengthening, and opportunities are harder to access and for whom.

It will investigate the stages between finding a vacancy and receiving an offer, including how applicants identify suitable roles, demonstrate their qualifications, obtain feedback and access employment support across borders. The distinction between the right to take a job and a realistic opportunity to find one is central.

AI-assisted applications and automated screening are possible influences to examine alongside employer practices, labour demand, entry-level requirements, language and differences between national services.

Comparative evidence

Analyse available labour-market and graduate-transition data, including unemployment duration and differences between countries and applicant groups. Map application processes and public employment services, supported by planned interviews with job seekers, employers and employment advisers.

Practical solutions

Assess options such as clearer vacancy information, simpler and more portable applications, better recognition and communication of qualifications, more transparent recruitment, and stronger connections between national employment services. Identify which problems require national reform and where common standards, interoperable services or coordinated support could improve opportunities across borders. Success should be assessed through access to suitable work, the time and burden of searching, and the quality of employment outcomes.

Different languages. Shared access.

This theme will investigate how language affects access to employment, public services and participation when people move across Europe. It will distinguish the demands of particular roles and services from barriers that accessible information or language support might reduce.

The central question is how wider access can coexist with Europe's linguistic diversity and what methods and practical solutions are available to eliminate barriers. The detailed scope and method are in development.

Stay connected. Across borders.

This theme will examine continuity in everyday communications when people move between member states: SIM cards, phone numbers, contracts and access to services linked to a national number.

It will ask where national arrangements create practical difficulties and what technical, regulatory or consumer-protection measures could address them. The detailed scope and method are in development.

About the author (opens in a new tab)

Independent research. Practical European cooperation.

The approach combines comparison of legal and policy frameworks with investigation of implementation. Legislation and official guidance establish the formal position; process mapping and planned empirical research examine the steps required in practice. Findings will distinguish documented rules, reported experiences and interpretations, making uncertainty explicit.

The intended outcome is a set of practical, evidence-based proposals. Options may include clearer information, simpler procedures, better connections between national services or common European arrangements. Their feasibility, costs, safeguards and likely effects must be assessed alongside the problem they seek to address.

Compare systems

Review national rules, institutional responsibilities and official procedures using a consistent set of questions.

Examine implementation

Trace real processes and investigate how administrative and organisational decisions affect people's options.

Develop proposals

Assess practical reforms and European coordination against evidence, feasibility and the needs of those affected.
